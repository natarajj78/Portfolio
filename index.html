Case study · Centrica — Business Travel Health Programme

Business Travel Health, made legible

A heuristic evaluation and redesign of the tool business travellers used to check health and safety requirements before a trip.

Brief

Centrica needed its business travel health programme to hold up under real conditions: travellers checking requirements at short notice, often on a phone, often stressed about a trip already underway. The existing tool buried that information behind dense navigation built for an internal audience, not a traveller.

The ask was straightforward to state and hard to do: make the right health and safety information findable in seconds, for a user who has never seen the tool before and won't again for months.

My role

Role: UX approach · persona development · heuristic evaluation · wireframe redesign

I owned the UX process end to end for this engagement: setting the approach, building the persona the redesign would be judged against, running the heuristic evaluation of the existing tool, and producing the wireframes that carried the fix through to a before/after comparison.

  • Defined the UX approach and evaluation criteria up front
  • Built the primary traveller persona from available research
  • Ran a structured heuristic evaluation of the live tool
  • Designed the replacement wireframes
  • Documented the before/after case for stakeholders

The challenge

The tool served two audiences with opposite needs — travel administrators who wanted comprehensive data, and travellers who wanted one answer, fast. Most of the existing interface had been built for the first audience and inherited by the second.

There was no budget for a rebuild. The fix had to work within the existing information architecture and technical constraints, which meant the redesign had to earn its improvements through hierarchy and wording, not new functionality.

What I did

  1. Set the UX approach: evaluate first, then redesign against evidence, not opinion
  2. Built a persona around the business traveller checking requirements under time pressure
  3. Walked the existing tool against a heuristic checklist, flagging friction by severity
  4. Prioritised the findings that would move the traveller experience most
  5. Sketched wireframes that resolved the top issues within the existing IA
  6. Paired each wireframe with the heuristic finding it addressed
  7. Packaged the work as a before/after comparison stakeholders could approve quickly

Heuristics on paper. Wireframes in Figma. Every fix traced back to a specific finding.

The outcome

  • A prioritised, severity-ranked list of usability issues stakeholders could act on immediately
  • A redesigned wireframe set that resolved the top-severity findings within the existing IA
  • A before/after artefact that made the case for the redesign without a rebuild

The shift wasn't a new feature — it was a tool a traveller could trust to answer one question fast, on the day it mattered.

What I'd do differently

The heuristic evaluation carried more of the weight than it should have. It's a fast, defensible way to find friction without a research budget, but it reflects expert judgement, not traveller behaviour. Given the choice again, I'd push for even a handful of moderated sessions with real travellers before finalising the wireframes — cheap insurance against fixing the wrong thing well.

The before/after framing also made it easy for stakeholders to approve the visible changes and skip the harder conversation about the underlying information architecture. That trade was right for the budget and timeline, but it left the deeper structural issue for a later project rather than solving it here.

How I worked

Evaluation first, opinions second. Every wireframe change traces back to a specific heuristic finding, which made stakeholder conversations shorter — the evidence did the arguing, not me.

Findings were shared as they emerged rather than saved for one big reveal, so nothing in the final before/after comparison was a surprise by the time it needed sign-off.
